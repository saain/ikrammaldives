Ash-Shamā'il · Entry 1

Ḥilyat Rasūlillāh ﷺ

His appearance, in the words of those who saw him

At a glance

The subjectThe physical description (ḥilyah) of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ — his form, face, hands, and bearing
Principal narratorsAnas ibn Malik, al-Bara' ibn ʿAzib, Jabir ibn Samurah, ʿAli ibn Abi Talib, Hind ibn Abi Halah, Umm Maʿbad (رضي الله عنهم)
Core textsal-Bukhari, Kitab al-Manaqib; Muslim, Kitab al-Fada'il; al-Tirmidhi, al-Shama'il al-Muhammadiyyah, opening chapters 1
Qur’ānic anchorsQ 33:21 (the beautiful example); Q 68:4 (his tremendous character); Q 94:4 (his mention raised high)
Principal sourcesal-Tirmidhi, al-Shama'il with its commentaries; Qadi ʿIyad, al-Shifa; al-Bayhaqi, Dala'il al-Nubuwwah

Summary

No man in history has been described by those who knew him as carefully as the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. The Companions preserved his form the way they preserved his words: his height and build, the colour of his skin, the length of his hair, the grey hairs that could be counted on fingers, the way he walked, the way he turned, the feel of his palm and the fragrance that followed him. These descriptions — the ḥilyah — fill the opening chapters of al-Tirmidhi's al-Shama'il and run through the sahih collections.

The agreed picture is of perfect balance. He ﷺ was neither tall nor short, neither pale nor dark, his hair neither curled nor straight — every report circles the same word: the middle way, in his very form. Asked whether his face was like a sword, al-Bara' (رضي الله عنه) answered: no — like the moon. Jabir ibn Samurah (رضي الله عنه) looked at him and at the full moon on a clear night, and found him more beautiful. Between his shoulders sat the Seal of Prophethood.

The fuller portraits — ʿAli's description ending "whoever saw him at first sight was filled with awe, and whoever mixed with him and knew him loved him"; the long ḥilyah of Hind ibn Abi Halah; Umm Maʿbad's words to her husband on the night of the Hijrah — complete a picture the ummah has studied for fourteen centuries: not to satisfy curiosity, but because knowing him ﷺ is the door to loving him, and loving him is the heart of faith.

The full study

1The narrations

The foundation texts are agreed upon. Anas ibn Malik (رضي الله عنه), who served the Prophet ﷺ for ten years, related:

"The Messenger of Allah ﷺ was neither conspicuously tall nor short; neither chalk-white nor deeply dark; his hair neither tightly curled nor lank. Allah commissioned him at the head of forty years; he remained in Makkah ten years and in Madinah ten; and Allah took him at the head of sixty, with not twenty white hairs in his head and beard." al-Bukhari, Kitab al-Manaqib · Muslim, Kitab al-Fada'il

Al-Bara' ibn ʿAzib (رضي الله عنه) related: "He was a man of medium build, broad between the shoulders, his hair reaching his earlobes. I saw him in a red garment — I have never seen anything more beautiful than him." (al-Bukhari; Muslim.) And when a man asked him, "Was his face like a sword?" he answered: "No — like the moon." (al-Bukhari, Kitab al-Manaqib.)

Jabir ibn Samurah (رضي الله عنه) said: "I saw the Messenger of Allah ﷺ on a clear night, in a red garment. I began to look at him and at the moon — and to me he was more beautiful than the moon." 2 Kaʿb ibn Malik (رضي الله عنه), describing the day his repentance was accepted, said: "When the Messenger of Allah ﷺ was pleased, his face would light as though it were a piece of the moon — and we knew that of him." (al-Bukhari; Muslim.)

2The picture in detail

The form

Of medium height, inclining toward tallness in company; 3 firmly built, broad-shouldered. His complexion was azhar al-lawn — luminous, white touched with redness (Anas, in al-Bukhari). His hair was between curled and straight, worn at times to the earlobes and at times nearer the shoulders — the reports describe him across twenty-three years of prophethood, and the scholars read their variety as precision, not contradiction. The grey hairs of his final years were so few that Anas (رضي الله عنه) said those who looked could count them.

The face

The moon narrations above belong here, and with them the smile: ʿAbdullah ibn al-Harith (رضي الله عنه) said, "I never saw anyone who smiled more than the Messenger of Allah ﷺ." (al-Tirmidhi.) Jabir ibn Samurah described his eyes as ashkal — and when the narrator Simak ibn Harb was asked what that meant, he explained: touched with lines of red. (Muslim, Kitab al-Fada'il.) 4

The hand and the fragrance

Anas (رضي الله عنه) said: "I never touched silk or brocade softer than the palm of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, and I never smelled musk or ambergris sweeter than his scent." (al-Bukhari; Muslim.) Umm Sulaym (رضي الله عنها) would collect his sweat as he slept at her house, and when he ﷺ asked her why, she answered: "It is your sweat; we put it in our perfume, and it is the sweetest of perfumes." (Muslim.)

The Seal of Prophethood

Between his shoulders, nearer the left, sat the khatam al-nubuwwah — a raised seal the size of a pigeon's egg (Jabir ibn Samurah, in Muslim). The young al-Sa'ib ibn Yazid (رضي الله عنه), taken to the Prophet ﷺ as a boy, remembered standing behind him and seeing it with his own eyes (al-Bukhari).

The fuller portraits

Three longer descriptions complete the picture. ʿAli (رضي الله عنه) would describe him and end: "Whoever saw him at first sight was filled with awe of him, and whoever mixed with him and came to know him loved him. The one who describes him can only say: I never saw, before him or after him, anyone like him ﷺ." (al-Tirmidhi, in the Sunan and the Shama'il.) 5

Hind ibn Abi Halah (رضي الله عنه), son of Khadijah (رضي الله عنها) and stepson of the Prophet ﷺ, gave the longest ḥilyah at the request of his nephew al-Hasan ibn ʿAli (رضي الله عنهما) — a portrait moving from his radiance "like the moon on its fullest night" through his manner of speaking, walking and turning. 6

And on the road of the Hijrah itself, Umm Maʿbad al-Khuzaʿiyyah described to her husband the stranger who had blessed her flock: radiant of face, beautiful of form, "more handsome and striking from afar, and sweeter and finer from near" — a description the books of dala'il preserve as one of the treasures of the sirah. 7

His bearing

He walked with purpose, lifting his feet as though descending a slope; when he turned to address someone, he turned with his whole body. He was mostly silent, spoke concisely and without affectation, and his companions' descriptions return always to the same pairing — an awe that struck the stranger, and a love that held everyone who knew him.

3Where the reports differ

The mould of this series holds here as in the campaigns: where the sources differ, we say so. The hair is described both at the earlobes and nearer the shoulders — different moments across two decades. The count of his grey hairs is given variously, all under twenty. The fuller portraits of Hind ibn Abi Halah and Umm Maʿbad reach us through chains the muhaddithun grade as weak in isolation; the ummah has nonetheless received them, because their content agrees in every essential with what the sahih collections establish. The footnotes carry the detail; the agreed core — the balanced form, the moonlit face, the Seal, the awe and the love — rests on al-Bukhari and Muslim throughout.

4What the scholars drew

Preservation as love, and as proof. Qadi ʿIyad built al-Shifa on the premise that knowing the Prophet's ﷺ person and rank is itself an act of devotion — and the scholars note that no founder of any community was documented by eyewitnesses in this way. The ḥilyah is part of the ummah's certainty about the one who brought the Book.

Balance in his very form. The narrations circle moderation: neither tall nor short, neither pale nor dark, hair neither curled nor straight. The scholars read in this an outward sign of the inward wasatiyyah — the middle way — that marks everything he ﷺ brought.

Awe, then love. ʿAli's closing line is treated by the commentators as a summary of prophetic presence: majesty (haybah) at first sight, intimacy (mahabbah) upon acquaintance — the two responses the believer holds together toward him ﷺ forever.

Knowing him in order to love him. The Prophet ﷺ said: "None of you believes until I am more beloved to him than his father, his child, and all mankind." (al-Bukhari; Muslim.) The scholars of the Shama'il tradition — from al-Tirmidhi to the commentators of every century — presented the ḥilyah as the nourishment of that love: you can hardly love in the abstract; the Companions loved a face and a voice, and left us their memory of both. He ﷺ also said: "Whoever sees me in a dream has truly seen me, for Shaytan cannot take my form." (al-Bukhari; Muslim.) The ḥilyah is how the ummah has known that form.

5Living this knowledge

The fruit of this entry is not information but salawat: the believer who has sat with these descriptions says صلى الله عليه وسلم differently. The ummah's artists turned the ḥilyah into calligraphy rather than portraiture — the description written beautifully, never drawn — and that restraint is itself part of the adab this entry teaches: he ﷺ is preserved for us in words, in Sunnah, and in the love of those who follow him.

6Sources and further reading

Primary

  • al-Tirmidhi, al-Shama'il al-Muhammadiyyah — the opening chapters on his appearance; several English translations are in print
  • al-Bukhari, Sahih, Kitab al-Manaqib (the chapters describing the Prophet ﷺ)
  • Muslim, Sahih, Kitab al-Fada'il
  • al-Bayhaqi, Dala'il al-Nubuwwah

Later and secondary

  • Qadi ʿIyad, al-Shifa bi-Taʿrif Huquq al-Mustafa
  • Ibn Hajar al-Haytami, Ashraf al-Wasa'il ila Fahm al-Shama'il (commentary on the Shama'il)
  • Ibn Kathir, Shama'il al-Rasul (drawn from al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya)

Notes

  1. Al-Tirmidhi's al-Shama'il al-Muhammadiyyah gathers some four hundred narrations in fifty-odd chapters, beginning with the chapters on his appearance ﷺ. Citations in this entry are given at collection and chapter level. ↩
  2. Transmitted by al-Tirmidhi in the Sunan and the Shama'il. Its meaning is corroborated by the agreed narrations of al-Bara' and Kaʿb ibn Malik (رضي الله عنهما) comparing his face to the moon. ↩
  3. The detail that he ﷺ seemed the tallest of a company he walked in comes from the longer portraits (see notes 5–6), not from the agreed short descriptions, which give only "neither tall nor short." ↩
  4. Some transmitters explained ashkal as wideness of the eye; Simak's explanation — redness as fine threads — is the one carried in Muslim, and the commentators prefer it. ↩
  5. ʿAli's description reaches al-Tirmidhi through chains including Ibrahim ibn Muhammad, of the household of ʿAli, whom the critics discuss; the closing sentences are the most widely received part and are corroborated in their meaning by the agreed narrations. ↩
  6. The ḥilyah of Hind ibn Abi Halah is transmitted in the Shama'il through a chain the muhaddithun grade as weak; it has nonetheless been received and expounded by the commentators for centuries because its content agrees with the established descriptions. We cite it as received tradition, not as independent proof. ↩
  7. The account of Umm Maʿbad is carried in the books of dala'il and sirah (al-Bayhaqi and others) through chains of similar standing — famous, beloved, and used by the scholars in the same received manner. ↩
