Ghazwat Badr al-Kubrā

The Great Battle of Badr · Friday, 17 Ramadan 2 AH

At a glance

DateFriday, 17 Ramadan 2 AH / March 624 CE 1
LocationThe wells of Badr, c. 130 km south-west of Madinah, on the Syria–Makkah caravan road
The two sidesThe Muslims of Madinah under the Messenger of Allah ﷺ · Quraysh of Makkah under Abu Jahl (ʿAmr ibn Hisham)
Strengthc. 313 men, 2 horses, 70 camels · c. 950–1,000 men, 100 horses, 700 camels 2
OutcomeDecisive Muslim victory; c. 70 Quraysh killed, c. 70 captured; 14 Muslims martyred
Qur’ānic passagesSūrat al-Anfāl (Q 8), esp. 8:5–19, 8:41–44, 8:67–71; Q 3:123; Q 54:45
Principal sourcesIbn Hisham, al-Sirah al-Nabawiyyah; al-Waqidi, Kitab al-Maghazi; al-Bukhari, Kitab al-Maghazi; Sahih Muslim

Summary

In the second year after the Hijrah, a Muslim column of a little over three hundred men set out from Madinah to intercept a great Quraysh caravan returning from Syria under Abu Sufyan ibn Harb. The caravan carried the trade wealth of Makkah — much of it built on property the Muhajirun had been forced to abandon. Abu Sufyan learned of the plan, swung his caravan to the coast road, and sent to Makkah for help; Quraysh marched north nearly a thousand strong. The caravan escaped, but Abu Jahl insisted the army continue to Badr to make a show of force before Arabia.

The Prophet ﷺ, now facing an army rather than a caravan, consulted his companions. The Muhajirun answered well, and al-Miqdad ibn ʿAmr (رضي الله عنه) declared they would never say as Banu Isra'il had said to Musa, "Go, you and your Lord, and fight — we are sitting here." The Ansar, whose pledge at ʿAqabah had been to defend him within Madinah, extended it freely to the field through their spokesman.3 At Badr the Muslims took the wells on the counsel of al-Hubab ibn al-Mundhir (رضي الله عنه), and the Prophet ﷺ spent the night in prayer.

On the morning of 17 Ramadan the two forces met. After single combat between three champions of each side, the general engagement broke Quraysh within hours. Abu Jahl fell, along with much of the leadership that had persecuted the believers in Makkah; some seventy were killed and seventy taken prisoner, against fourteen Muslim martyrs. The prisoners were treated with a mercy their own kin acknowledged — fed from their captors' own bread, many ransomed, some by teaching Madinan children to write.

The Qur’ān names the day Yawm al-Furqān, the Day of the Criterion (Q 8:41): the day the small community of Madinah became a power that Arabia could no longer ignore.

The full study

1Background

The Hijrah did not end the conflict between the Muslims and Quraysh; it changed its shape. The Muhajirun had left homes, trade and property in Makkah, and Quraysh had seized what they left behind. Revelation had by now given the believers permission to fight those who fought them — "Permission [to fight] has been given to those who are being fought, because they were wronged" (Q 22:39) — and through the first eighteen months in Madinah the Prophet ﷺ sent a series of small expeditions (sarāyā) toward the Makkan caravan routes: reconnaissance, pressure on Quraysh's economic artery, and treaty-making with the coastal tribes.

In Jumada al-Akhirah of 2 AH, a richly laden Quraysh caravan had passed north to Syria beyond the Muslims' reach. Its return was another matter. Under Abu Sufyan ibn Harb with only thirty to forty guards, it carried — al-Waqidi puts the figure at fifty thousand gold dinars — the pooled trading capital of virtually every house of Makkah. The Prophet ﷺ called for volunteers to intercept it; because the aim was a lightly guarded caravan, the muster was quick and modest, and no general mobilisation was made.

2The march and the consultation

The column left Madinah in the first half of Ramadan: a little over three hundred men, with seventy camels ridden in turns — the Prophet ﷺ sharing his mount like any other man — and two horses.

Abu Sufyan, a careful merchant, was questioning travellers along the route. Learning of the Muslim movement, he did two things: he hired Damdam ibn ʿAmr al-Ghifari to ride hard for Makkah crying that the caravan was in peril, and he turned his own train west to the coast road, abandoning the Badr watering stop. Makkah answered Damdam's alarm with nearly its full fighting strength — some nine hundred and fifty men, a hundred horses, and the chiefs of every clan except Abu Lahab, who sent a substitute. When word reached the army that the caravan had slipped away safely, some counselled return; Abu Jahl refused: they would camp at Badr, slaughter camels, drink and hear the singing-girls, "so that Arabia will hear of us and fear us ever after." 4

News reached the Prophet ﷺ that he now faced not forty guards but an army. He gathered the people and asked their counsel. Abu Bakr (رضي الله عنه) spoke well, and ʿUmar (رضي الله عنه) after him. Then al-Miqdad ibn ʿAmr (رضي الله عنه) rose:

"O Messenger of Allah, go where Allah has shown you and we are with you. By Allah, we will not say to you as Banu Isra'il said to Musa — 'Go, you and your Lord, and fight; we are sitting here' — but: go, you and your Lord, and fight, and we will fight beside you." al-Bukhari, Kitab al-Maghazi

Still the Prophet ﷺ said, "Advise me, O people" — and by this, the narrators explain, he meant the Ansar, for their pledge at ʿAqabah bound them to defend him within their city, and they were the majority of the column. Their spokesman answered:3

"It seems you mean us, O Messenger of Allah. We have believed in you and testified that what you brought is the truth… Go forward with the blessing of Allah — for by Him who sent you with the truth, if you led us into this sea we would plunge into it with you."

The Prophet's ﷺ face lit, and he said: "March, and good tidings — for Allah has promised me one of the two parties" (cf. Q 8:7).

The consultation is not decoration in this story. A defensive pact was extended to open battle by free consent, sought and given before a single sword was drawn.

3The two forces

At Badr the Muslims numbered a little over three hundred and ten 2 — Muhajirun and Ansar together, with boys turned back along the road and ʿUthman ibn ʿAffan (رضي الله عنه) excused in Madinah at the sickbed of his wife Ruqayyah (رضي الله عنها), the Prophet's ﷺ daughter; he was later allotted a full share and counted among the people of Badr (al-Bukhari).

Of Quraysh's strength the Prophet ﷺ made a soldier's estimate. A water party captured two of the enemy's water-carriers; asked how many camels the army slaughtered daily, they answered nine one day and ten the next. "The people are between nine hundred and a thousand," he said — a hundred men to a camel. (Ibn Hisham, al-Sirah.)

4The course of the battle

The wells. The Muslims reached Badr first and camped at the nearest water. Al-Hubab ibn al-Mundhir (رضي الله عنه) asked a question that the scholars have treasured since: "O Messenger of Allah — is this a position Allah has revealed to you, so that we may not advance or retreat from it, or is it opinion, war and stratagem?" It was opinion, war and stratagem, he ﷺ replied. Then, said al-Hubab, this was not the place: advance to the well nearest the enemy, hold it, build a cistern, and deny them the water. The Prophet ﷺ rose and did exactly as his companion advised. (Ibn Hisham, al-Sirah.) 5 At Saʿd ibn Muʿadh's (رضي الله عنه) suggestion a shelter of palm branches was raised for the Prophet ﷺ on a rise overlooking the field.

The night. Rain fell — heavy on the Quraysh side, mud and hindrance; light on the Muslim side, settling the sand and filling the cisterns. The Qur’ān recalls it: "…and He sent down upon you water from the sky to purify you thereby… and to make firm your feet" (Q 8:11). A light sleep came over the believers as a security from Him (Q 8:11), while the Prophet ﷺ stood the night in prayer beneath a tree.

The morning. He ﷺ straightened the ranks with an arrow and returned to the shelter with Abu Bakr, and there pleaded with his Lord: "O Allah, fulfil for me what You have promised. O Allah, if this small band perishes today, You will not be worshipped on the earth." He pressed the du’ā until his cloak fell from his shoulders and Abu Bakr, taking his hand, said: enough, O Messenger of Allah — your Lord will fulfil His promise. The answer stands in the Book:

"[Remember] when you sought help of your Lord, and He answered you: 'I will reinforce you with a thousand of the angels, rank upon rank.'" Q 8:9 · the account in Sahih Muslim

Single combat. Three champions of Quraysh strode out — ʿUtbah ibn Rabiʿah, his brother Shaybah, and his son al-Walid — and demanded their equals. Three young men of the Ansar stepped forward; the challengers waved them off: "Noble equals from our own kin." The Prophet ﷺ sent his own house: ʿUbaydah ibn al-Harith, Hamzah ibn ʿAbd al-Muttalib, and ʿAli ibn Abi Talib (رضي الله عنهم). In Ibn Ishaq's account, ʿUbaydah — the eldest — took ʿUtbah; Hamzah took Shaybah; ʿAli took al-Walid.6 Hamzah and ʿAli killed their men at once; ʿUbaydah and his opponent each struck the other down, and Hamzah and ʿAli turned and finished ʿUtbah, carrying their wounded kinsman back. ʿUbaydah (رضي الله عنه) died of the wound at al-Safraʾ on the homeward march — the first of the Prophet's ﷺ own clan to fall in battle in the path of Allah.

The engagement. The lines closed. The Prophet ﷺ took a handful of pebbles and cast it toward Quraysh — and of this the revelation says: "You did not throw when you threw, but Allah threw" (Q 8:17). He ﷺ had commanded restraint until the enemy pressed close, and forbidden his companions from seeking out certain men — among them those of Banu Hashim marched out against their will. The angels fought that day by the text of the Book (Q 8:9, 8:12), and the Companions related signs of it that the books of maghāzī preserve.

Within hours the Quraysh centre broke. Abu Jahl, the Firʿawn of this ummah as the Prophet ﷺ called him, was cut down by two young men of the Ansar and found at his last breath by ʿAbdullah ibn Masʿud (رضي الله عنه).7 Umayyah ibn Khalaf — the man who had tortured Bilal (رضي الله عنه) in Makkah — was recognised and killed despite an attempt to hold him for ransom (al-Bukhari). The rest fled south, leaving their dead on the field.

5Aftermath

Some seventy of Quraysh were killed and a like number captured (Sahih Muslim); the fallen included the greater part of the leadership that had directed the persecution in Makkah. Twenty-four of them were dragged to a dry well at Badr, and the Prophet ﷺ stood over it by night and addressed them by name: "Have you found what your Lord promised you to be true? For I have found what my Lord promised me true." To ʿUmar's astonished question he ﷺ replied: "You do not hear what I say better than they — but they cannot answer." (al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim.)

Fourteen Muslims were martyred — six of the Muhajirun and eight of the Ansar. Among them was the young Haritha ibn Suraqah (رضي الله عنه), struck by a stray arrow as he drank at the cistern. His mother came to the Prophet ﷺ: if her son was in Paradise she would bear it with patience; if not, Allah would see what she would do of weeping. He ﷺ answered: "O Umm Haritha — they are gardens within gardens, and your son has attained the highest Firdaws." (al-Bukhari.)

Two riders, Zayd ibn Harithah and ʿAbdullah ibn Rawahah (رضي الله عنهما), carried the news ahead to Madinah — arriving as the city buried Ruqayyah (رضي الله عنها). In Makkah, Abu Lahab, who had not marched, died within days of the news reaching him. Two prisoners alone were executed on the return road for crimes beyond the battlefield — al-Nadr ibn al-Harith and ʿUqbah ibn Abi Muʿayt, foremost among those who had tortured the believers and sought the Prophet's ﷺ life in Makkah.

A dispute over the spoils among the victors became the occasion for the opening of Sūrat al-Anfāl: "They ask you about the spoils. Say: the spoils are for Allah and the Messenger — so fear Allah and set right what is between you" (Q 8:1), and the fifth (khumus) was ordained for Allah and His Messenger, the kinsfolk, the orphans, the needy and the wayfarer (Q 8:41).

6The captives

On the fate of the seventy prisoners the Prophet ﷺ again consulted. Abu Bakr (رضي الله عنه) counselled ransom: they were kin, and Allah might yet guide them to Islam. ʿUmar (رضي الله عنه) counselled the sword against the leaders of disbelief. The Prophet ﷺ inclined to Abu Bakr's mercy and accepted ransom — and revelation then came affirming the gravity of the choice (Q 8:67–69), a passage the scholars of tafsīr treat as a divine instruction in the weighing of mercy against firmness, ending nonetheless in the words "so eat of what you have taken as lawful and good, and fear Allah."

The treatment of the captives became part of the story Makkah itself told. They were distributed among the Companions with the command to treat them well; Abu ʿAziz ibn ʿUmayr, one of the prisoners, later recalled that his captors gave him their bread and kept dates for themselves, so that he was ashamed (Ibn Hisham, al-Sirah). Ransoms were set according to means; the poor were freed without payment; and those who could read and write were freed on teaching ten children of Madinah to write (Ibn Saʿd, al-Tabaqat; Musnad Ahmad) — a price that tells its own story about what the new community valued. When Zaynab (رضي الله عنها), the Prophet's ﷺ daughter still in Makkah, sent for the ransom of her husband a necklace that had belonged to Khadijah (رضي الله عنها), the Prophet ﷺ was deeply moved, and with his companions' consent the man was released and the necklace returned (Abu Dawud, Kitab al-Jihad).

7The revelation

Sūrat al-Anfāl (Q 8) is the sustained Qur’ānic commentary on Badr, and reading it against the events is itself a study in how revelation and sīrah illuminate one another:

  • Q 8:5–8 — the march, the reluctance of some, and the promise of "one of the two parties": the caravan or the army.
  • Q 8:9–12 — the plea for help, the thousand angels, the light sleep, the rain, and firmness cast into believing hearts.
  • Q 8:17 — the handful of pebbles: "you did not throw when you threw."
  • Q 8:41 — the khumus, and the naming of the day: Yawm al-Furqan, "the day the two hosts met."
  • Q 8:42–44 — the providential ordering of positions, and each side made to seem few in the other's eyes "so that Allah might accomplish a matter already decreed."
  • Q 8:45–47 — the standing orders of the believers: firmness, much remembrance of Allah, obedience, no quarrelling, and the pointed contrast with those who "went forth from their homes in insolence, to be seen of men" — Quraysh with their feasting and singing-girls.
  • Q 8:67–71 — the captives.

Beyond al-Anfal: Q 3:123 — "Allah gave you victory at Badr when you were weak" — recalls the day within the discourse on Uhud; and Q 54:45, "The assembly will be routed and will turn their backs," revealed in Makkah years earlier, was recited by the Prophet ﷺ at Badr as its meaning stood before the believers' eyes (al-Bukhari).

8Lessons drawn by the scholars

The classical scholars — Ibn al-Qayyim devotes a section of Zad al-Maʿad to the fiqh of Badr — return to certain themes:

Consultation as method, not gesture. The Prophet ﷺ consulted before committing the army, and again over position, and again over the prisoners — while receiving revelation. If shūrā bound him ﷺ, the scholars ask, what of those after him?

Revelation and expertise distinguished. Al-Hubab's question — "revelation, or opinion and stratagem?" — and the Prophet's ﷺ acceptance of his counsel established for the ummah that worldly means are the domain of knowledge and experience, and that asking the question is adab, not impertinence.

Means, then tawakkul. The wells were seized, the cisterns built, the ranks dressed — and then the Prophet ﷺ stood the night begging his Lord. Neither substitutes for the other.

Consent, not compulsion. The Ansar's pledge was defensive; its extension to the open field was sought and freely given. The scholars read here the sanctity of covenant terms even under the pressure of war.

Mercy with power. Captives fed before captors, freedom priced in literacy, a necklace returned. The conduct of the victors of Badr became da’wah in itself — several of the prisoners later embraced Islam.

The criterion. Yawm al-Furqan: the day drew the line not merely between two armies but between two eras — and the Qur’ān locates the victory not in numbers or arms but in Allah's aid to a patient, obedient, united band (Q 8:45–47), a standing lesson the khutaba' of every century have renewed.

9Notable participants

The people of Badr — al-Badriyyun — hold the highest rank among the Companions. When the Companion Hatib ibn Abi Baltaʿah (رضي الله عنه) later erred gravely, the Prophet ﷺ said: "He was present at Badr — and what do you know? Perhaps Allah looked upon the people of Badr and said: do as you will, for I have forgiven you." (al-Bukhari; Sahih Muslim.) Jibril himself asked the Prophet ﷺ how the people of Badr were counted among the Muslims; "among the best of them," he ﷺ replied — "and so are the angels who were present at Badr among the angels" (al-Bukhari).

Among those present: Abu Bakr, ʿUmar, ʿAli, Hamzah, Muʿadh ibn Jabal, Saʿd ibn Muʿadh, Bilal, ʿAmmar ibn Yasir, and ʿAbdullah ibn Masʿud (رضي الله عنهم أجمعين). ʿUthman (رضي الله عنه) was counted among them by the Prophet's ﷺ own assignment though excused from the field. The fourteen martyrs are listed by name in Ibn Hisham; ʿUbaydah ibn al-Harith and Haritha ibn Suraqah (رضي الله عنهما) are noticed above.
